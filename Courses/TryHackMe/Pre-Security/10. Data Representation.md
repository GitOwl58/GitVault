How do computers represent colours?

If a computer were limited to 8 colors, it would only need to indicate which color is "switched on" and which is "switched off." It can use three digits of 1 and 0 to represent the states of red, green, and blue. For example, 111 would be all 3 lights switched on, while 100 would be only the red switched on. This digit, which can be either 1 or 0, is called a **[[Bit]]**.

![[RGB.png]]

|Color Representation|Meaning|Color Name|
|---|---|---|
|`000`|All colors are off|Black|
|`001`|Only blue is on|Blue|
|`010`|Only green is on|Green|
|`100`|Only red is on|Red|
|`011`|Green and blue are on|Cyan|
|`101`|Red and blue are on|Magenta|
|`110`|Red and green are on|Yellow|
|`111`|All colors are on|White|

Being limited to eight colors is inconvenient, as we prefer millions of colors. If each of the 3 lights (red, green, blue) had 256 levels instead of just 2 (on or off): 256 × 256 × 256 = 16,777,216 — that's more than 16 million colors, covering most of our needs.

One bit represents 2 states: on and off. We need 8 bits to express 256 states. A group of 8 bits is referred to as a **[[Byte]]** (also **octet**).

1 Byte = 8 Bits = 1 Octet

## [[Binary]] Numbers

`1001` = 1×2³ + 0×2² + 0×2¹ + 1×2⁰ = 8+0+0+1 = **9**

`0000` = 0 · `0001` = 1 · `0010` = 2 · `0011` = 3

## Conclusion
- **Decimal (Base-10)**: The system we use in everyday life.
- **[[Binary]] (Base-2)**: Computers understand two states, encoded as `0` and `1`.
- **[[Hexadecimal]] (Base-16)**: Every 4 binary digits (bits) can be grouped as one hex digit, ranging `0`–`F`.
- **Octal (Base-8)**: Every 3 bits can be grouped as one octal digit, ranging `0`–`7`. Less commonly encountered.

We also learned how a color can be represented:
- **[[Bit]]**: short for binary digit, either `0` or `1`.
- **[[Byte]]**: On modern systems, 8 bits. Also referred to as an octet.
- **Hex color**: A color as a combination of red, green, blue. One byte per primary color gives 16+ million combinations.
